30 day money back guarantees and what they actually cover

The guarantee is the shop's promise. Consumer law is separate, longer, and usually stronger. Most refund arguments come from mixing the two up.

On this page

    A 30 day money back guarantee is a marketing promise, not a legal right, and the two are governed by completely different rules. Knowing which one you are relying on decides whether you get a refund, a credit note, or an argument.

    Company policy and consumer law are separate

    Consumer law gives you rights when something is faulty, misdescribed or not fit for purpose. Those rights have their own time limits, and in most systems they run far beyond 30 days. A money back guarantee is something the seller offers on top, usually covering the case where the product works perfectly and you simply do not want it.

    The practical result: if your blender is broken on day 40, the expired guarantee is irrelevant. You are claiming under the law, not the policy. If your blender works and you changed your mind on day 40, the guarantee was your only route and it has closed.

    The cooling off right is why the shorter 14 day return window appears on so many online orders. That one is law. The 30 day version is the shop being generous, and it can be withdrawn, shortened or hedged with conditions.

    When the 30 days starts counting

    Three different start points appear in real policies, and the difference matters more than the length.

    • From delivery. The fairest version, and the one consumer law uses. A slow courier does not eat your window.
    • From purchase or dispatch. Common on marketplaces. A 10 day delivery leaves you 20 usable days.
    • From first use or activation. Used by software and subscriptions, and by anything with a registration step.

    Check whether the deadline is the date you notify the seller or the date the goods arrive back at the warehouse. Policies that require the item to be received within 30 days effectively give you about three weeks, because shipping eats the rest.

    Notify in writing, then ship. An email on day 28 fixes your claim date even if the parcel takes a week to arrive. A phone call with no record does not.

    The conditions that void most guarantees

    The headline is 30 days. The exclusions are where refunds die.

    Original packaging and condition

    Many policies require the item unused and in resaleable condition. Under UK and EU cooling off rules you are allowed to handle goods as you would in a shop, so opening a box does not end your right, though the seller can reduce the refund if you went further than that.

    Restocking fees

    Legal in the United States for change of mind returns, commonly 10 to 25 percent on electronics and furniture. Not permitted against the statutory cooling off right in the UK and EU.

    Who pays return shipping

    Usually you, unless the item was faulty. On a 40 kg treadmill, the return freight can exceed the point of returning it, which is why bulky goods often come with generous sounding windows.

    Excluded categories

    Perishables, personalised items, sealed media and hygiene products are excluded almost everywhere, and the law usually agrees with the seller on these.

    Software, subscriptions and the pro rata question

    For SaaS and digital goods, the 30 day guarantee is normally full refund inside the window and nothing after it, with no pro rata for the unused part of an annual plan. Some providers refund only the current billing period. A few refund nothing once you have downloaded or streamed the content, which is permitted in the EU when you agreed to immediate delivery and waived the cooling off right, and the checkbox that did that is usually at checkout.

    Annual plans are where money gets stuck. Cancelling on day 45 of a 365 day plan usually stops renewal and nothing else. The cheaper protection is to start on a monthly plan and switch to annual after the guarantee window closes on both.

    The other 30 days: notice periods

    The same number governs most rolling contracts. Gym memberships, mobile plans, insurance, storage units and residential tenancies commonly require 30 days notice to end, and almost all of them count calendar days from receipt of written notice rather than from your next billing date.

    This produces the classic complaint: you cancel a gym on the 2nd, the billing runs on the 5th, and you are charged again because the 30 days has not expired. The charge is usually correct under the contract. Cancel a full billing cycle plus a few days ahead.

    Where the contract is written in months rather than days, a 30 day period and a calendar month are not identical, which matters for anyone on a four weekly billing cycle. Longer commitments shift to 60 day notice for commercial leases and 90 day notice for senior employment and some landlord obligations.

    If you are close to the deadline

    1. Find the exact start date on the order confirmation or delivery receipt, not your memory.
    2. Email the seller today, stating the order number and that you are exercising the guarantee.
    3. Keep the packaging until the refund has cleared.
    4. Photograph the item and the shipping label before it leaves you.
    5. If the window has closed, switch arguments. Ask whether the product is faulty or was described accurately, because that puts you back under consumer law with a much longer clock.

    Common questions

    Does a 30 day money back guarantee have to be honoured by law?

    Once it is advertised as part of the offer, it generally forms part of the contract and the seller has to honour its terms. The length itself is chosen by the seller, not set by law.

    Does the 30 days run from ordering or from delivery?

    It depends on the policy wording. Statutory cooling off rights run from delivery, but voluntary guarantees sometimes run from the purchase date, which shortens the usable window.

    Can a shop charge a restocking fee within the 30 days?

    In the United States yes, and 10 to 25 percent is common on electronics and furniture. Against the statutory 14 day cooling off right in the UK and EU, no.

    What if the item is faulty after 30 days?

    The guarantee window is not the limit for faults. Consumer law gives separate rights that run for years in most systems, covering repair, replacement or a partial refund.
